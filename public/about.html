Magnetospheric Radiometric Cybernetic Garden

An interactive 3D visualization of Earth's magnetic field and magnetosphere, driven by real geophysical models and historical solar wind observations. All physics runs in the browser — no server, no plugins.

How It Works

The visualization is computed in three layers, stacked from the inside out:

1
Internal field — IGRF-14
Earth's main magnetic field is computed from the International Geomagnetic Reference Field (IGRF-14), a degree-13 spherical harmonic model published by NOAA/IAGA. The app evaluates the magnetic potential at each point and integrates B(r,θ,φ) using fourth-order Runge–Kutta (RK4) to trace field lines from seed points on the surface. The spherical harmonic degree slider lets you see how successive multipoles add complexity to the pure tilted dipole. Coefficients for every IGRF epoch from 1900 to 2025 ship with the app and are interpolated to the simulation date as the timeline moves, so the field's slow secular drift (including the wandering magnetic poles) is reproduced; beyond 2025 the model extrapolates using the published secular-variation rates.
2
External field — Tsyganenko T01
Solar wind interaction reshapes the field significantly: dayside field lines are compressed by the solar wind ram pressure; nightside lines are stretched into the long magnetotail. The T01 empirical model (Tsyganenko 2002) captures all major current systems — ring current, magnetotail current sheet, Birkeland currents, Chapman-Ferraro compression, and IMF penetration — in a single closed-form evaluation. It is parameterized by dynamic pressure, Dst, IMF By/Bz, and the G1/G2 storm-history indices from the Qin-Denton database. The Shue (1998) model defines the magnetopause boundary. Field lines are traced until they return to Earth or leave the magnetopause, which can carry them 100 RE down the tail. T01 was fitted to spacecraft data in the near magnetosphere (within roughly 15 RE on the nightside), so the shape of the far tail is an extrapolation of the model rather than a fitted result — in particular, lines that close through the distant tail would in reality be open lobe field lines. The inputs come from the manual sliders, from one of three presets (Quiet, Moderate Storm, Severe Storm), or — in Historical Data mode — from the timeline playback described below.
3
Historical data playback — OMNI2 + WGhour.d
The timeline at the bottom drives the solar wind inputs from real hourly observations spanning 1963 to the present. Data comes from the NASA OMNI2 merged dataset (solar wind speed, density, IMF By/Bz, Dst) combined with G1 and G2 pre-computed from the Qin-Denton database using Robert Weigel's QinDenton Fortran tool. Monthly JSON files (~28 KB each) are lazy-loaded as you navigate, so only the months you visit are fetched.
Radiation belts — The Van Allen belts are drawn from a compact analytic model of relative trapped-particle flux in a dipole field, f(L, λm), where L = r / (RE cos²λm): a peaked profile in L, multiplied by the fall-off of flux along a field line away from the magnetic equator, and cut off just above the atmosphere. Each belt is rendered as three nested iso-flux shells, and when a clipping plane is active the cut face is filled with the same flux map — the crescent cross-sections familiar from flux maps of the AE-8/AP-8 models and from NASA's Van Allen Probes graphics (e.g. NASA SVS 30470). The Colors control switches between a single rainbow colormap for all belts (blue edge to red core) and a separate hue for each belt. This is a shape model for visualization, not a flux forecast. It is calibrated so that the middle contour encloses the high-flux core of each belt — inner belt L ≈ 1.3–1.9, peaking near L ≈ 1.5; outer belt L ≈ 3.0–4.8, peaking near L ≈ 4 — while the faint outer shell shows the wider trapped region around it (to L ≈ 2.3 and L ≈ 6). The outer belt's outer edge moves outward by up to 0.8 RE at high geomagnetic activity (Kp ≥ 7), following the observed boundary motion (Ganushkina et al. 2011). During strong storms (Dst < −50 nT and Kp ≳ 3) a third belt fades in at L ≈ 3.0–3.5 — the storage ring of ultra-relativistic electrons discovered by the Van Allen Probes (Baker et al. 2013; Shprits et al. 2013) — and the outer belt's inner edge retreats to L ≈ 4, opening a second slot between them as observed in September 2012. The ring shares the Outer Belt visibility toggle and disappears as conditions quiet. Colour also carries intensity: an empirical Kp/Dst estimate of each belt's flux moves its colours up or down the colormap (or brightens its hue). Separate L-shell isosurfaces use the internal field only; the dipole L approximation breaks down with external fields in the tail and at the magnetopause.
Sharing & bookmarking — The URL updates automatically as you change settings. Every parameter — date, solar wind values, belt visibility, camera angle — is encoded in the URL fragment (the part after #), so no server state is involved. Copy the URL from your browser to share or bookmark any configuration. Only values that differ from the defaults are written, keeping shared URLs short. Pasting a URL into a new tab restores the full simulation state exactly.

The Magnetosphere and Solar Weather

Earth's magnetic shield

Earth's magnetic field is confined into a teardrop-shaped cavity — the magnetosphere — by the continuous outflow of magnetized plasma from the Sun known as the solar wind. Blowing at 300–800 km/s, the solar wind carries its own embedded magnetic field, the interplanetary magnetic field (IMF). Where the solar wind first encounters Earth's field, a standing shock wave called the bow shock forms at roughly 13–15 RE on the dayside. The boundary where Earth's magnetic pressure balances the solar wind ram pressure is the magnetopause, located at roughly 10–11 RE under quiet conditions. On the nightside the field cannot balance the dynamic pressure, and is dragged out hundreds of RE downstream into the long magnetotail, with two lobes of nearly antiparallel field separated by a thin current sheet.

This asymmetry is visible in the field line tubes in this visualization: dayside lines are short and tightly arched; nightside lines are drawn far into the tail. The magnetopause shape itself can be displayed via Solar Wind → Show Magnetopause, and the bow shock via Show Bow Shock — drawn with its standoff at a fixed 1.36× the magnetopause standoff, a representative value for typical solar wind Mach numbers (cf. Farris & Russell 1994), and a slightly more open flare.

The key driver: IMF Bz

The most important parameter governing how strongly the solar wind couples to Earth is the north-south component of the IMF (Bz). When Bz is northward — parallel to Earth's dayside field — coupling is weak and the magnetosphere is relatively closed. When Bz turns southward, the antiparallel field geometry at the magnetopause nose enables magnetic reconnection: field lines break and re-connect across the boundary, opening a direct channel for solar wind plasma and energy to flow into the magnetosphere. Sustained southward Bz is the single greatest predictor of geomagnetic storm severity, and is the primary input that drives ring current inflation as measured by the Dst index.

Reconnection timescales — how fast field lines change topology

Not all field line changes happen at the same speed. When solar wind conditions shift gradually — ram pressure rising, the magnetopause slowly compressing — field lines that remain topologically closed simply reshape. Magnetic flux opened by dayside reconnection is carried over the poles, stored in the tail lobes and eventually closed again by reconnection in the magnetotail: the circulation proposed by Dungey (1961), which takes of the order of an hour or more to complete (see Cowley 1984 for a review). But the moment a field line passes through the reconnection site — the X-line, where antiparallel fields meet — its topology changes abruptly. The reconnection region itself is thin, comparable to the ion inertial length (of order 100 km at the magnetopause), and the change in connectivity is communicated along the field at the Alfvén speed, reaching the ionosphere within a few minutes. Compared with the convection timescale, an individual field line's change of topology is nearly instantaneous. The first in-situ evidence of this process at the magnetopause came from accelerated plasma flows seen by the ISEE spacecraft (Paschmann et al. 1979).

How this appears in the visualization. When solar wind parameters change and the field-line tracing is recomputed, lines that remain topologically closed (both footpoints on Earth's surface) morph slowly over ~8 s, conveying the gradual convection of stable flux tubes. Lines that change topology — a previously closed line opening to the solar wind, or an open polar-cap line closing as the dayside field relaxes — snap to their new configuration in ~0.8 s, roughly 10× faster. The snap uses an ease-in curve (slow start, rapid finish) to suggest the physical behaviour: a field line resists topological change, then breaks and reconnects suddenly. The 0.8 s and 8 s durations are visual design choices, not physical simulations of reconnection or convection timescales — the real phenomena span seconds to hours. The aim is to convey the character of the two processes: gradual reshaping versus sudden topological snap.

Field lines versus L-shell surfaces

Two distinct representations appear in this visualization, answering different questions:

①
Field line tubes (Show Field Lines in the control panel) trace the actual path of the magnetic field vector B(r), computed by fourth-order Runge–Kutta integration of the combined IGRF-14 + T01 field. They show the magnetic highways along which particles gyrate and bounce. With solar wind enabled the tubes are visibly asymmetric: compressed and arched on the dayside, stretched into the nightside tail. Each line is followed to its natural end — back to Earth's surface, or out through the magnetopause — so polar lines run far down the tail; zoom out to see them. The shape responds in real time to the solar wind sliders and to historical data during playback. A Color by Solar Wind Influence toggle recolors each tube by how much of the local field is external rather than internal — a red gradient highlighting where the solar wind, not Earth, dominates.
②
L-shell surfaces (the transparent isosurfaces under Isosurfaces → L-shell field topology; the radiation belts are drawn in the same coordinate) show surfaces of constant L — a parameter that approximates the equatorial distance (in RE) at which the local field line would cross the equatorial plane in a dipole field. These surfaces represent drift shells: a trapped particle does not stay on a single field line but drifts azimuthally around Earth, sweeping through a full ring of field lines. L tells you which drift orbit the particle occupies, not which field line it sits on. These surfaces are computed from the internal (IGRF) field using the dipole L approximation, so they keep their smooth dipole shape even with solar wind enabled — but grid cells outside the magnetopause are excluded, so during severe storms the outermost shells are visibly cut off where they cross the compressed dayside boundary. A second isosurface mode renders contours of field strength |B| (500–10,000 nT) instead of L.
Cutaway views — The Clipping controls slice the scene with an equatorial or meridional plane (the meridional cut angle is adjustable). Clipping the belts meridionally exposes their cross-sections, filled with the flux map — the classic crescent-shaped belt diagram from textbooks, here computed live from the dipole geometry. An equatorial cut shows the same map as concentric rings.
Limitation of the L approximation. McIlwain's L is properly defined from the field strength and the bounce invariant of a trapped particle. This app uses the simpler dipole estimate L = r / (RE cos²λm), with the magnetic latitude λm taken from the local field direction. It is a good approximation in the inner magnetosphere (L < 4) but degrades in the distorted outer belt and magnetotail where the field topology departs strongly from a dipole. The rigorous equivalent, Roederer L*, requires tracing the full drift orbit in the 3D field — computationally expensive and outside the scope of this real-time app (Roederer & Lejosne 2018).

Magnetopause shadowing

When the compressed magnetopause crosses inside the outermost drift shells, electrons on those shells encounter the boundary on every azimuthal drift pass and escape into the solar wind. This process — magnetopause shadowing — can remove most of the relativistic electrons from the outer part of the belt within hours, producing the flux "dropouts" seen early in many storms; outward radial transport then feeds electrons from lower L toward the boundary as well (Herrera et al. 2016; Staples et al. 2022). In this visualization, with the Severe Storm solar wind preset enabled, the outermost L-shell isosurfaces (Isosurfaces → L-shell mode) are cut open on the dayside, leaving an incomplete torus: the open region represents drift shells that particles cannot maintain without crossing the magnetopause and being lost. The belt recovers when the solar wind pressure relents and new particles are injected on the nightside.

Whether a given storm ultimately enhances or depletes the outer belt depends on which process dominates — injection and wave acceleration, or loss to the magnetopause and atmosphere. This stochastic competition is an active area of research and explains why no two geomagnetic storms produce the same radiation belt outcome.

Radiation Belt Particle Physics

What the particle animation shows — and what it doesn't. The animated dots represent the azimuthal drift of trapped charged particles around Earth — the slowest of three nested periodic motions. They do not show individual particle trajectories. In reality, each particle simultaneously gyrates around a field line — thousands to hundreds of thousands of times per second for electrons — bounces back and forth between mirror points roughly once a second or faster, and drifts around Earth in anything from a minute to hours. Only the drift is animated here because it is the motion that builds the ring current and determines the large-scale structure of the radiation belts.
Physics note — What are field lines, really?
Are magnetic field lines physical objects, or a visualization device?

Field lines are mathematical objects — specifically, the integral curves of the vector field B: the family of curves whose tangent at every point equals the local field direction. Formally each line satisfies dr/ds = B̂(r). The field B is physical — it carries energy, exerts forces, and obeys Maxwell's equations. The lines are one way to represent that continuous field visually. Maxwell's law ∇·B = 0 (no magnetic monopoles) does constrain field-line topology: because B has zero divergence, lines must form closed loops or extend to infinity and cannot terminate at point sources. This is genuine physics. But the lines themselves are not discrete physical objects — they are a graphical choice. Michael Faraday introduced "lines of force" as a way of representing the direction and strength of the magnetic force from point to point. In MHD plasmas, Alfvén's frozen-in theorem lends field lines a quasi-material identity — plasma in a highly conducting fluid cannot cross them — but Alfvén himself later cautioned against taking the picture literally, and it fails wherever magnetic reconnection occurs.

When iron filings align in a magnetic field, do they reveal pre-existing lines?

No — each filing independently samples the continuous field, and the visible chains are an emergent collective phenomenon. The mechanism has two stages. First, ferromagnetic induction: each rod-shaped filing acquires an induced dipole moment aligned with the local B at its position, then rotates until its long axis lies along the field. This is an independent local measurement of a continuous field, not a snapping-to of a pre-existing groove in space. Second, chain formation: adjacent aligned filings attract each other end-to-end via dipole–dipole coupling and link up. The "lines" you see are those chains — their number and spacing are determined by filing density and initial placement, not by any intrinsic discreteness of the field. A denser sprinkling produces more, closer chains; in the continuum limit the pattern would become a smooth surface.

Particles gyrate "around a field line" — but if magnetism is a continuous force, what are they orbiting?

There is no wire. The gyration geometry arises entirely from the Lorentz force F = q(v × B), which acts perpendicular to both the particle velocity and the local field direction. For a particle moving in a uniform field, the perpendicular velocity component traces a circle with Larmor (cyclotron) radius rL = mv⊥ / (|q|B) and angular frequency ωc = |q|B / m — derived purely from the vector cross product, with no physical structure to orbit.

In a slowly varying field (one that changes little over one Larmor radius — the adiabatic condition), the particle trajectory can be decomposed into fast gyration plus the slow drift of the gyration centre, called the guiding center. The guiding center tracks along the mathematical field line — the integral curve of B — to lowest order. This is the guiding-center approximation, developed by Hannes Alfvén and formalized by Northrop (1963). A particle "orbiting a field line" is shorthand for: the guiding center advances along the integral curve of B while the particle executes rapid circles around it — the curve provides no physical support, only geometric definition.

The three adiabatic invariants (magnetic moment μ, longitudinal invariant J, and drift-shell flux Φ) that underpin the structure of the Van Allen belts all arise from this guiding-center picture. They are conserved quantities of the Lorentz-force motion averaged over successively longer timescales — not properties of physical "lines." See Northrop (1963) and Roederer (1970) for the complete derivation.

Three nested periodic motions

Every trapped particle executes three independent periodic motions, each faster than the next:

1
Gyration — around a magnetic field line, at the particle's Larmor radius.
Period: about ten microseconds to a fraction of a millisecond for a 1 MeV electron (a gyroradius of under a kilometre near Earth, about 10 km in the outer belt); milliseconds to tenths of a second for a 10 MeV proton (tens to hundreds of kilometres). Far too small and fast to animate — gyration is averaged over and not shown.
2
Bounce — back and forth along the field line between magnetic mirror points in each hemisphere, where the increasing field strength reflects the particle.
Period: about 0.1 second to a few seconds. In the visualization, bounce is represented by a static spread in magnetic latitude (λm) up to each particle's mirror point — particles are distributed along field lines but do not oscillate, since the drift is the physically significant motion for the belt structure.
3
Azimuthal drift — slowly around Earth due to the gradient and curvature of the dipole field.
Period: about a minute (30 MeV inner-belt protons) to ~16 minutes (1 MeV electrons at L = 4) to hours (100 keV ring-current ions). The drift is faster at higher energy and at larger L, and at a given energy is nearly the same for electrons and protons until the particle becomes relativistic.
This is what the animation shows. Electrons drift eastward (counterclockwise viewed from the north pole); protons drift westward (clockwise). The drift periods in the animation are accelerated roughly 6× from physical rates so the motion is clearly visible.

The ring current

The opposite drift directions of electrons and protons constitute the ring current — a toroidal, westward electric current circling Earth at roughly L = 2–7, carried mainly by ions of tens to hundreds of keV. It is not a current of individual charges moving in circles; it is the net statistical drift of many particles whose gyration and bounce average out, leaving only the slow azimuthal motion. The Dst geomagnetic index directly measures the ring current: more negative Dst means a stronger ring current and a more disturbed magnetic field at Earth's surface. During a geomagnetic storm the ring current typically depresses the equatorial surface field by 50–400 nT; the March 1989 storm reached −589 nT.

Storm-time injection

In quiet times the radiation belts are slowly replenished from the solar wind and from substorm activity. During geomagnetic storms, the compressed magnetotail on the nightside (midnight sector) injects large populations of plasma-sheet particles inward, rapidly energising them and enhancing the ring current. New particles in this visualization therefore appear on the nightside (the side of Earth facing away from the Sun) and then drift around to fill the belt — matching the real injection geometry. The injection rate and target L-shell both scale with the Dst index.

Four particle populations — what the visualization shows

The inner and outer belts are not populated by a single species. The visualization models four physically distinct populations, each with a different source, species, energy, and drift direction. They are distinguished by colour: orange = protons, blue = electrons.

A
Inner belt protons — CRAND source (L = 1.3–1.9)
Cosmic Ray Albedo Neutron Decay: galactic cosmic rays (the most energetic particles in the universe) continuously bombard Earth's upper atmosphere. They produce cascades of secondary neutrons. Some of those neutrons escape upward and then decay in flight — n → p + e− + ν̄e — creating a proton and an electron that may be magnetically trapped. The resulting protons accumulate deep in the inner belt. This source is constant and independent of solar wind — it runs whether the Sun is active or quiet. Orange particles, drifting quickly westward (clockwise from the north pole): at 30 MeV one circuit of Earth takes only about a minute, so even at the animation's ~6× speed-up they visibly race around the inner belt. Very long visual lifetimes (300–600 s).
B
Inner belt electrons — inward diffusion (L = 1.5–1.9)
Electrons from the outer belt slowly diffuse inward through radial diffusion, driven by ULF wave activity. They accumulate in the outer portion of the inner belt (L = 1.5–1.9) but rarely reach the deep inner belt (L < 1.5) where the proton population dominates. This is a small, steady population — independent of the current storm state. Blue particles, drifting eastward (counterclockwise) and far more slowly than the colocated CRAND protons: at L = 1.5 a 1 MeV electron takes about 40 minutes to circle Earth, a 30 MeV proton about one. For a particle mirroring at the equator of a dipole field the drift period is T = 4π q B0 RE² / (3 L γβ² mc²), which reduces to T ≈ 44 min / (L·EMeV) for non-relativistic particles of either species (Schulz & Lanzerotti 1974; Walt 1994). When viewed together, the overlapping orange and blue glow of populations A and B produces a warm, mixed-colour inner belt — a visible signature of two species coexisting.
C
Outer belt electrons — storm injection (L ≈ 3.0–4.5)
During geomagnetic storms, the compressed nightside plasma sheet injects large fluxes of electrons inward. These electrons are quickly energised by betatron and Fermi acceleration during inward transport, and further accelerated over hours to days by chorus wave-particle interactions. This is the most dynamic population: outer belt fluxes can change by orders of magnitude over hours to days, rising through injection and acceleration and falling through magnetopause shadowing and wave-driven precipitation into the atmosphere. Blue particles appearing on the nightside and drifting rapidly eastward.
D
Ring current protons — storm injection (L ≈ 2.5–4.5)
Injected alongside the outer belt electrons from the nightside plasma sheet, these are the 10–300 keV protons that are the ring current — the same current whose depression of Earth's surface field we measure as the Dst index. The Dst is, in effect, a direct measurement of the energy content of these protons. They are injected deeper than the outer belt electrons — compressing to L ≈ 1.5–3.0 in severe storms — and, crucially, are not blocked by the slot region — protons can exist freely in the slot (L = 2–3). Orange particles drifting westward, slowly (a 100 keV proton takes about two hours to circle Earth), with injection rising with storm intensity.
What to look for in the visualization:
  • Inner belt colour mix — the deep inner belt (L < 2) glows orange (CRAND protons) with a blue wash from diffusion electrons. The combined additive glow appears warm-white at high particle density.
  • Drift speed contrast — watch the inner belt closely: orange 30 MeV protons race westward while blue 1 MeV electrons creep eastward in the same region, passing through each other. This is not a simulation artefact — drift speed scales with energy, and the direction depends on the sign of the charge.
  • The slot gap — in quiet conditions (Dst near 0), the region between L ≈ 2 and L ≈ 3 is conspicuously dark of electrons. Electrons there are scattered into the atmosphere by whistler-mode waves, chiefly plasmaspheric hiss, faster than they are resupplied. Orange ring current protons may still pass through the slot.
  • Storm-time injection — scrub the timeline to a storm event (e.g. May 2024). Watch outer belt electrons (blue) appear on the nightside and sweep eastward, filling the outer belt within minutes of simulation time. The target L-range shifts inward as Dst drops.
  • Inner belt stability — however fierce the storm, the CRAND proton population (A) barely changes in this visualization. Inner-belt protons are lost very slowly — over years to centuries, depending on energy and L — so the belt's core is nearly immune to individual storms (the largest storms can still erode its outer edge).
  • Belt colour — the outer belt's colours climb the colormap during storms (its core turning from yellow-orange to red), encoding the elevated electron flux from an empirical Kp/Dst estimate motivated by the activity dependence reported by Meredith et al. (2003).

Aurora connection

Particles whose equatorial pitch angle falls below the loss cone angle (αLC, a few degrees at large L) are no longer magnetically trapped. They spiral down field lines into the upper atmosphere at 80–300 km altitude, colliding with oxygen and nitrogen atoms and producing the aurora. The foot of a dipole field line at L ≈ 6–10 reaches the Earth's surface at roughly 66–72° geomagnetic latitude (cos²Λ = 1/L) — the auroral oval. Storm-time particle loss into the loss cone therefore appears as a brightening aurora at high latitudes. The aurora oval in this visualization brightens with the magnitude of the Dst index.

Live coupling to historical solar wind data

The particle injection rate and the aurora brightness are not fixed — they respond in real time to the Dst geomagnetic index drawn from the historical solar wind record. As the timeline advances, the app reads hourly Dst values from the same OMNI2-derived dataset that drives the magnetic field model, so the belt and aurora activity you see reflects what was actually happening in near-Earth space at that moment.

During a severe storm the injection zone shifts markedly inward — particles flood down to L = 2, close to the inner belt — and the aurora oval intensifies strongly. After the storm passes, the excess population slowly decays through wave-particle scattering on timescales of tens of seconds in the outer belt and many minutes in the inner belt (accelerated from real lifetimes of days to years for visual clarity).

The most dramatic belt and aurora activity corresponds to large geomagnetic storms. Use the timeline bar at the bottom of the screen to navigate to dates with historically intense activity. Some notable events in the dataset:

  • March 1989 — one of the strongest storms of the modern era (Dst ≈ −589 nT), responsible for the Quebec power-grid blackout. View in visualization ↗
  • October–November 2003 ("Halloween storms") — two back-to-back X-class flares with Dst reaching −383 nT; aurora visible at mid-latitudes across North America and Europe. View in visualization ↗
  • September 2017 — X9.3 flare (the largest of Solar Cycle 24), Dst ≈ −148 nT in the dataset used here; aurora seen from mid-latitudes. View in visualization ↗
  • May 2024 — the first G5 ("extreme") storm since the 2003 Halloween storms, Dst ≈ −406 nT; widespread aurora across the northern and southern hemispheres. View in visualization ↗

Each link above opens the visualization near the storm's peak with belts, particles, and aurora already enabled. Set the playback speed to 60× or 3600× to watch the belt fill and drain over hours. The Dst readout in the Solar Wind panel updates automatically as the timeline advances.

Satellite Visualization

The app displays a curated catalog of approximately 1,500 real Earth-orbiting satellites, propagated in real time using the official US Space Force SGP4 orbit propagator. Satellites are sourced from the Space Track catalogue and selected to represent a physically meaningful cross-section: major science missions, navigation constellations, weather satellites, and representative members of megaconstellations (Starlink, OneWeb, Iridium), while excluding debris, rocket bodies, and unidentified fragments.

Positions are computed inside a Web Worker — keeping the render loop frame-rate–locked — and smoothly interpolated between propagation ticks so orbital motion looks continuous. Selecting a satellite displays its L-shell, magnetic field magnitude, and radiation belt region in the environment overlay, connecting the population of real spacecraft to the hazardous space weather environment they operate in.

Orbit classification

Space Track definitions applied from TLE line-2 fields, tested in priority order — HEO first, then LEO, GEO, MEO (so a highly elliptical orbit is classed HEO even if its mean motion would also satisfy another rule):

Class Criterion Examples
HEO Eccentricity > 0.25 Molniya, Tundra, IBEX, Chandra, MMS, Van Allen Probes
LEO Mean motion > 11.25 rev/day, eccentricity < 0.25 ISS, Hubble, Starlink, Sentinel, Landsat
GEO Mean motion 0.99–1.01 rev/day, eccentricity < 0.01 GOES, Meteosat
MEO Orbital period 600–800 min, eccentricity < 0.25 GPS, GLONASS, Galileo, BeiDou
OTHER Everything else (sub-orbital, super-GEO, transitional) —

SGP4 propagation pipeline

1
TLE catalog: Two-line element sets are downloaded from space-track.org (US Space Force 18th Space Defense Squadron). A curated subset of ~1,500 satellites is generated by scripts/convert-satellites.js from a 3-line element snapshot, with monthly historical TLE snapshots produced by scripts/convert-tles.js from Space Track's bulk archive.
2
Worker initialisation: On enable, all TLE pairs are parsed by the SGP4 WASM propagator (TleAddSatFrLines_wasm → Sgp4InitSats_wasm). The worker holds the entire SGP4 state in WASM heap memory. When the simulation crosses a month boundary, the matching monthly TLE snapshot is loaded and the worker is reinitialised with fresher elements, minimising propagation error over long time spans.
3
Propagation: Sgp4PropDs50UtcPosVel_wasm computes ECI (TEME, z-up, km) position and velocity for all satellites in one batch call. Time is expressed as days since 1950-01-01 00:00:00 UTC (Ds50UTC), matching the propagator's internal epoch convention. Propagation is throttled to match the simulation speed: 10 s at 1×, down to 200 ms at 86,400×.
4
Coordinate conversion: ECI positions are rotated to ECEF using Greenwich Mean Sidereal Time (Vallado & McClain 2013, Eq. 3-47), then mapped to BeltViz scene coordinates (Y-up, Earth radius = 1). Between propagation ticks, positions are linearly interpolated every frame so orbital motion appears continuous.
5
Orbit trace: Selecting a satellite triggers a 181-point orbit trace propagated at period / 180 minute steps, producing one full revolution. The trace is drawn on over 2 seconds using BufferGeometry.setDrawRange(), then held while the satellite remains selected.
